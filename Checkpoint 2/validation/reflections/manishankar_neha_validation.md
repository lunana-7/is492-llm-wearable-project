## 1. Testing Notes (Concept Validation)

For this checkpoint, I focused on understanding how students might react to professors using AI-powered smart glasses to remember names and student information.

- **Accuracy & Reliability:** Both simulated participants emphasized that incorrect facial recognition could make interactions worse. This reinforced the importance of showing information only when the system is confident.
- **Latency & Performance:** Both participants felt that professors pausing for several seconds to read information would make conversations awkward. The glasses need to provide reminders quickly and without interrupting eye contact.
- **Privacy & Consent:** Both participants wanted control over their personal information and were concerned about unwanted tracking or the system being connected to grading and attendance.
- **Cost & Efficiency:** Both questioned whether expensive hardware would be worthwhile just for remembering student names. The system needs to provide enough value to justify its cost.
- **Human–AI Teaming:** Both preferred professors to verify information rather than automatically trust the AI.

These observations are based on simulated interview scenarios rather than measured prototype performance.

## 2. Interview Insights

### Interviewee 7 — Senior Mechanical Engineering Student (25–120 Students)

- **Context:** Fourth-year engineering student who generally likes technology but values accuracy and efficiency.
- **Key Insights:**
  - **Accuracy:** Concerned that frequent recognition mistakes would make the glasses less useful than simply asking students for their names.
  - **Reliability & Latency:** Felt that inconsistent recognition or waiting several seconds for information would make interactions awkward.
  - **Privacy:** Wanted student-controlled profiles, private opt-out options, and guarantees that information would not be connected to grading or attendance.
  - **Cost:** Questioned whether expensive hardware would be worth purchasing unless it significantly improved interactions.
  - **Human–AI Teaming:** Believed professors should verify AI-generated information instead of trusting it automatically.

**Main Takeaway:** This scenario helped me recognize that technology being innovative does not automatically make it practical. ContextLens needs to be accurate, fast, and genuinely useful.

### Interviewee 8 — First-Year Psychology Student (150–250 Students)

- **Context:** First-year student who values personal interactions with professors and is more cautious about AI in education.
- **Key Insights:**
  - **Human Connection:** Felt more comfortable asking questions when instructors remembered their name and expressed concern about technology making interactions less personal.
  - **Privacy & Consent:** Worried about facial recognition, constant monitoring, and feeling pressured to opt in.
  - **Accuracy:** Preferred professors to ask for a student's name rather than rely on an incorrect AI prediction.
  - **Latency:** Felt that delays would make conversations unnatural.
  - **Cost & Alternatives:** Suggested that seating charts and professors making more effort to learn names might be simpler alternatives.

**Main Takeaway:** This scenario showed me that ContextLens must solve a meaningful problem without making students feel watched or replacing genuine relationships.

## 3. Personal Reflection

### What Surprised Me

One thing that stood out to me was how differently students might react to the same technology. The engineering student was generally open to the idea but focused on whether it would actually work well enough to justify the cost. The psychology student was more concerned about privacy and whether using facial recognition would make classroom interactions feel less personal.

Before working through these scenarios, I mostly thought about ContextLens as a helpful tool for professors who have trouble remembering names, especially in larger classes. I didn't think as much about whether students would actually want to participate or how the technology could change the way professors interact with them.

### How My Thinking Changed

The biggest thing I learned was that AI does not always need to provide an answer. If the system is unsure about someone's identity, it would be better to display nothing than risk giving a professor the wrong name.

I also realized that student consent should be a major part of our design rather than something we add at the end. Even if the glasses are accurate, students may still feel uncomfortable knowing that a camera is being used in the classroom.

As an Information Sciences and Data Science student, I tend to think about how we can improve accuracy and make technology more efficient. This checkpoint pushed me to think beyond the technical side and consider whether the system would actually make people feel more comfortable and respected.

### Human–AI Complementarity

The Gonzalez et al. (2026) complementarity framework helped me understand that AI should support human abilities instead of replacing them.

For ContextLens, AI would be responsible for retrieving student-approved information, while professors would remain responsible for communicating with students, verifying information, and building relationships.

I think this balance is important because students want professors to genuinely recognize them, not just read their names from a screen.

### What I Would Change Moving Forward

- **Prioritize Accuracy Over Automatic Recognition:** If the system is uncertain, it should not guess.
- **Give Students More Control:** Students should decide what information is shared and be able to opt out privately.
- **Keep Interactions Natural:** Reminders should appear quickly without distracting professors.
- **Reduce Unnecessary Dependence:** The glasses should help professors learn names rather than rely on the technology permanently.
- **Evaluate Practical Value:** We should compare the glasses with simpler alternatives, such as seating charts, to determine whether the technology is worth the cost.

### Final Takeaway

Overall, this checkpoint helped me realize that a successful AI system needs to do more than function correctly. It also needs to be useful, trustworthy, and comfortable for the people affected by it.

My biggest takeaway is that **ContextLens should help professors remember students while preserving the personal connections that make students feel recognized and valued.**
